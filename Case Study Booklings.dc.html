Booklings

Reading tracker · UX/UI, research & prototype

A reading tracker I would actually keep using

I read a lot and I run a book club with friends, so I have tried to keep track of what I read. Goodreads is where everyone ends up, and its interface feels a decade behind the reading itself. The apps that look more modern borrow their motivation from productivity software instead: streaks that break, counters that shame, rankings that compare. Booklings is my attempt at the opposite, a tracker that is cozy to open and uses gamification as an invitation to log a book rather than as pressure to perform.

Research

A streak motivates until the day it breaks

Streaks are the default motivation mechanic in habit software, and they have a well-documented failure mode: once a run is long enough to feel valuable, missing a single day makes people abandon the habit rather than restart from zero. That finding shaped most of what follows.

The evidence

  • Streaks shift from motivator to obligation as they lengthen. The longer the run, the worse breaking it feels.
  • When the streak goes, the habit often goes with it: people quit outright rather than tapering.
  • Duolingo's Streak Freeze cut churn by relieving anxiety, not by adding pressure.

What it changed in the design

  • The garden, not the streak, is the main progress surface: a flower you planted stays planted.
  • Nothing in the app resets to zero. Coins are earned and never expire; beds persist season to season.
  • No leaderboards and no friend comparison anywhere in the IA.
  • The streak card is a doorway to the garden, not a scoreboard. It invites, it does not warn.

Design principles

The rules, and where you can see each one

Progress accumulates, never resets

A flower you planted stays planted; coins never expire. Stopping for three weeks costs you nothing.

Garden · Home streak card

Compare yourself to no one

No leaderboards, no friend feed, no social ranking anywhere in the structure. The only comparison offered is to your own previous months.

Stats · Books per month

Let mood pick the book

Discovery is emotional, not algorithmic: a wrapped blind pick with a handful of hints, or a quiz about how you feel tonight.

Blind pick · Mood quiz

Warmth is structural

Sage and cream, a serif for every title, hand-drawn flowers and real covers. The comfort is in the system, not applied as decoration.

Every screen

Ideate

Every mechanic has a brake

XP & levels

Every logged session earns XP, and levels are named tiers rather than scores: 1,480 XP, Level 7 Bibliophile, 120 short of Archivist.

Brake · XP comes from pages read, never from opening the app.

The yearly challenge

One target, set by the reader (24 of 40 books in 2026), carried as a ring on Home and again in Stats.

Brake · Editable at any time, including downwards.

Coins

Finishing a book pays coins; 340 sit in the wallet. It is the only currency, and the Shop takes nothing else.

Brake · Nothing is paywalled: coins buy decoration, never access.

The garden

Coins buy seeds, and each finished book grows a flower in one of the beds: what a reading year looks like a month later.

Brake · Plants never wilt and never die from absence.

Badges

Badges are earned quietly, and locked ones stay visible as dashed rings with the condition written underneath.

Brake · None is time-limited, so none of them creates urgency.

Blind pick & mood quiz

Two ways to be handed a book instead of choosing one: a wrapped parcel with a few hints, or a short quiz about how tonight feels.

Brake · Opt-in, and neither records a wrong answer.

The loop

Read → log pages → XP and coins → a level, or a new seed → the garden visibly changes → a reason to open the app tomorrow that isn't a number about to reset.

Prototype

A structure where one destination is a verb

A row of tabs plus a raised centre action. The + is not a screen but a sheet, and it branches into every way a book can enter the library, with discovery sitting alongside search rather than buried under it.

Final design

Every flower in the garden is drawn by hand

A short palette and two typefaces carry the whole product. Ink green for text and the deepest surfaces, sage for the accents and the garden, card and paper for everything underneath, a range narrow enough that nothing ever shouts. Crimson Pro sets the titles, giving book covers and screen headings the same literary weight; Josefin Sans handles the interface, geometric and rounded enough to stay soft at small sizes.

Warmth is structural, so the illustration cannot be borrowed. Each plant, bed and shop item was drawn for the app: sage and cream, soft edges, no stock assets.

Takeaways

What I kept, what I cut, what comes next

The hardest part was not drawing the flowers or building the screens: it was deciding which mechanics earned their place. Almost everything on the gamification menu works in the short term, so each mechanic I kept had to answer one question: does this make someone want to log a book, or only anxious about not having logged one? Streaks, leaderboards and time-limited badges came out. XP, coins, the yearly challenge and the garden stayed, each with a constraint written next to it.

Without a client, nobody was going to tell me the scope was too big. Cutting the social layer was the clearest case: it is the feature my own book club would use first, and still the biggest open question. Relatedness is the need this version deliberately does not serve, and I would rather name that gap than half-build it.

Next steps, in order: test the prototype with my book club and a few readers outside it, to see whether removing the streak keeps people logging or just removes a motivator some of them wanted. Then the first days, which this case study skips: an empty shelf, no coins, no flowers, and making that feel like a beginning rather than a blank page. Then the social layer, built so it never turns into a ranking. And a garden with more than one bed: seasons, collections, flowers that only appear under certain conditions.
